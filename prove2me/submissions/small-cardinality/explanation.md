# Why every counterexample has at least ten points

The published counting theorem first shows that a nonempty point set in
strictly convex position with four equidistant witnesses at every vertex has
at least nine points; the common radius may vary from vertex to vertex. The
published finite theorem then rules out the remaining nine-point case. These
two results force any counterexample to have at least ten points.

The second theorem states the same consequence in the direction used by the
mission: every nonempty point set in strictly convex position with at most
nine points contains a vertex that does not have four other points at one
common positive distance.

The counting input follows Dumitrescu's 2006 isosceles-count argument, with
the later cap refinements credited in its own published proof. The exact
nine-point exclusion and this final combination are this project's Lean
formalization.

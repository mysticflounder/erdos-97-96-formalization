1       Introduction
A classical conjecture of Erdős [6, 7] asserts that every set of n points in convex position in the plane
contains a point that determines at least ⌊n/2⌋ distinct distances to the remaining points (Problem
#982 [3]). Since every vertex of the regular n-gon determines exactly ⌊n/2⌋ distinct distances,
the conjecture states that fconv (n) = ⌊n/2⌋, where fconv (n) denotes the largest integer such that
every such set contains a point determining at least fconv (n) distinct distances. Moser [12] proved
fconv (n) ≥ ⌈n/3⌉, while Szemerédi’s double-counting argument gives the essentially identical
bound fconv (n) ≥ ⌈(n − 1)/3⌉ (see [7]). Dumitrescu [5] obtained fconv (n) ≥ 13n/36 − O(1)        in 2006.

Nivasch, Pach, Pinchasi, and Zerbib [13] sharpened the bound to fconv (n) ≥ 36 + 22701 n − O(1),
which has remained the tightest standing benchmark for the problem since 2013. (See also [4].)
    The conjecture concerns the number of distinct distances measured from a single point. For
counting the distinct distances determined by all pairs of points, the convex case is settled: Altman
[2] proved that n points in convex position always determine at least ⌊n/2⌋ distinct distances in
total, while for arbitrary planar point sets the all-pairs problem was resolved up to logarithmic
factors by Guth and Katz [10]. For the single-point count, on the other hand, the conjectured

    The proof further refines the iteration method of [13], which we first review. An edge of the
point set is said to be good if its perpendicular bisector meets the set in at most one point; a

measured from either end increase monotonically. The support points of the smallest enclosing
circle divide the point set into at most three caps, and in a cap of t points at most t2 /4 pairs

Pinchasi, and Zerbib [13] delete the support points and recompute the smallest enclosing circle,
for a number of steps proportional to n. If every support point encountered stays within circular
distance a small constant times n of the original support set, then step i yields about n − 6i new
good edges incident to the points deleted at that step, and the steps together beat the baseline. If
instead some current support point escapes beyond that distance from all three original support
points, then a window of consecutive current vertices around it lies inside a single original cap,
hence is itself a cap, and Lemma 12 of [13] — in a cap of 2t consecutive vertices, at most 78 t2 + O(t)
of the t2 pairs joining the two halves are witnessed inside the cap — yields 18 t2 − O(t) additional
good edges; these straddle two current caps and are therefore disjoint from the baseline, which
in this case is recounted inside the current caps and corrected for the reinstated deleted points.

2.1    Caps and Witnesses
Definition 4 ([13, Definition 3]). A set Q = {x1 , . . . , xt } of points in convex position, listed in
cyclic order, is a cap with endpoints x1 and xt if there is a circle through x1 and xt such that Q
lies in the closed region bounded by the chord x1 xt and an arc of that circle of length at most a
semicircle.
    As observed in [13], Q is a cap if and only if ∠x1 xi xt ≥ π/2 for all 1 < i < t, and, by convexity,
if and only if ∠xi xj xk ≥ π/2 whenever i < j < k. This characterization yields two facts that
we use repeatedly. First, every subset of a cap with at least two points is a cap; in particular,
every point of a cap is an endpoint of the sub-cap on either side of it. Second, distances from an
endpoint increase strictly,
                                   |x1 x2 | < |x1 x3 | < · · · < |x1 xt |,
since ∠x1 xi xi+1 is the largest angle of the triangle x1 xi xi+1 . Combining the two facts, the distances
from any point of a cap to the other points increase strictly as one moves away from it, on either
side.
     Caps enter the question at hand through an observation of Moser [12]: on a smallest enclosing
circle of a finite set S in convex position one can choose two or three support points of S dividing
the circle into arcs of length at most a semicircle, and these support points divide S into at most
three caps that meet only at the support points. Indeed, the points of S between two circularly
consecutive support points are separated by the chord joining them from the rest of S, and they
lie inside the enclosing circle, hence in the region bounded by that chord and an arc of at most a
semicircle.
Lemma 5 (A bad edge has a witness on each chain). Let u, v be vertices of a convex polygon. If
uv is bad, then one witness for uv lies on each of the two boundary chains from u to v, the two
paths into which u and v divide the boundary of the polygon. (See Figure 1.)
Proof. The perpendicular bisector of uv passes through the midpoint of uv, and its two rays from
this midpoint lie in the two open half-planes bounded by the line uv. Since the polygon is convex,
each ray meets the boundary at most once, and the two boundary chains from u to v lie in the
two corresponding closed half-planes. If the bisector contains two vertices of the polygon, these
vertices therefore lie on different chains.

     In what follows, S denotes a subset of P carrying the cyclic order inherited from P (in our
applications, S is P itself or a set obtained from P by deletions), and Q ⊆ S. Suppose now that a
cap Q, with endpoints a and b, consists of consecutive vertices of S. The interval spanned by Q is
the set of points of P — not merely of S — consisting of a, b, and the boundary chain of P from
a to b that contains Q \ {a, b}; it is linearly ordered from a to b, so “between” is unambiguous
inside it. We say that a witness lies in Q if it belongs to Q. By Lemma 5, an edge with both
endpoints in Q that is bad in S has an S-witness strictly between its endpoints, and that witness
lies in Q; conversely, an edge of Q with no witness in Q has at most one S-witness altogether and
is therefore good in S. Finally, for points lying in the interval spanned by Q, our notion of witness
coincides with the one used in [13] (a point on the perpendicular bisector not separated from Q by
the line ab). With this identification of witnesses, the cited results (Lemmas 6, 8, and 10) apply in
the equivalent forms stated below.

Lemma 6 (witness ordering; [13, Lemma 6]). Let Q ⊆ S be a cap with endpoints a and b, let
c ∈ Q, and let x, y ∈ S be witnesses for ac and for ab, respectively, neither separated from Q by

Corollary 7. Let Q be a cap consisting of consecutive vertices of S, and let x be a point of P
lying in the interval spanned by Q. Then the edges uv of Q such that |xu| = |xv| and x lies strictly
between u and v form a matching.
Proof. Suppose x witnesses two such edges sharing a vertex u. Since x lies strictly between u
and each of the other endpoints, those endpoints lie on the same side of u in the interval; so the
edges are uv and uv ′ with v strictly between u and v ′ , and x lies strictly between u and v, hence
strictly between u and v ′ . The points of Q from u to v ′ form a sub-cap with endpoints u and v ′
containing v; the point x, like the interior of this sub-cap, lies on the boundary chain of P from u
to v ′ inside the interval, so it is not separated from the sub-cap by the line uv ′ . Applying Lemma 6
with S = P , taking a = u, b = v ′ , c = v, and with x = y the common witness, gives x ̸= x, a
contradiction.

distcirc (p, X) = minx∈X distcirc (p, x) for the circular distance from p to the nearest point of X.

Remark 19. The proof uses the conservative residual estimate (n − 3m)2 /12, the quantity obtained
when at most three vertices are deleted at each of the m steps. With the imbalance credited in every
case, the aggregate-escape case yields exactly the target η by design. Both Lemma 16 and (22)

about 6.2 · 10−8 . The aggregate test gains over watching a single support point because the residual
invalidation is governed stepwise by a(η−EA )/3 , and universally by aη/3 , rather than by aη ; the
factor 3 is the number of support points, and a sharper upper bound in Lemma 10, replacing the
constant 78 by c < 87 , would raise η further.


A     Numerical Verification Certificate
The numerical inequalities used in Section 4 are all rational inequalities or one-dimensional
elementary analytic estimates. For reproducibility, the ancillary file verify_certificates.py
recomputes every displayed numerical certificate used in the proof, using only the Python standard
library. In particular, it verifies the uniform separation certificate in Lemma 16, the square-root
and arcsinh majorants used in the final Case 2 estimate, the exact Case 2 rational margin, the
assembly identity (18) for the deletion and residual terms, and the final conversion from good
edges to distinct distances.
    The certificate uses exact rational arithmetic via Python’s fractions.Fraction wherever the
claim is rational. The only transcendental comparison is the arcsinh majorant, which is certified
in the proof by an alternating Maclaurin truncation and is also checked numerically at high
precision in the script. The script additionally stress-tests the scheme-level inequalities, including
the cap-imbalance envelope EA ≥ E(ρ), the design identity (5) for the threshold aδ in (4), and the
